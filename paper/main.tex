\begin{abstract}
Exact verifiers (primality tests, SAT solvers, unit tests) label data with no noise at no marginal cost, and they supply the rewards of verifiable-reward post-training. We use them as a measurement instrument: with label noise removed, the effect of the supervision \emph{format} can be isolated. We fine-tune Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with LoRA on exactly labeled examples and compare supervision by the answer alone with supervision by a deterministic procedure trace followed by the answer. Answer-only training recovers about what simple surface probes recover: on 4-digit primality it reaches \primeA\% and coincides with the rule ``the last digit is 1, 3, 7 or 9'' on \primeAagreeLastDigitLo--\primeAagreeLastDigitHi\% of test items; on divisibility by 7 or 13 it stays at chance at every training size. Trace supervision teaches the procedure when each step is supervised often enough. Divisibility by 7 rises from \divsevenBnSixty\% at \nTrainSmall{} examples to \divsevenBnTwoSeventy\% at 270, and divisibility by 13 from \divthirteenBnOneEighty\% at \nTrain{} to \divthirteenBnFiveForty\% at 540. A preregistered hypothesis that per-step accuracy follows $m$, the number of supervised transitions per (remainder, digit) table entry, which is known before training, held in \SsevenHeld{} of the \SsevenScored{} predicted cells scored so far. It failed where the step is easier (divisibility by 11), and a dose test failed where the step is harder (13 trails 7 at equal $m$). A preregistered prediction that trace accuracy compounds a fixed per-step accuracy over length also failed. Controls show that the trace must be the input's own computation, must precede the answer, and is not replaced by prompting the base model. The lesson concerns supervision design for verifiable-reward pipelines, not arithmetic.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Verifiable-reward post-training rests on programs that decide correctness exactly: answer checkers for mathematics, unit tests for code, solvers for logic \citep{lambert2024tulu,deepseek2025r1}. Such verifiers are often described as a source of perfect labels for free. They are perfect in one sense: a verified label is never wrong. Whether a model trained on those labels learns the computation the verifier performs, or a cheaper function that agrees with it on the training distribution, is a separate question, and in practice it is confounded with label noise, with task difficulty, and with the format of the supervision.

We use exact verifiers to remove the first confound. When every label is correct, any difference between two training regimes on the same inputs is a difference in what the supervision exposes. We choose small decision tasks whose structure is known in advance (which surface rules approximate the label, which steps the natural procedure takes) so that what should be learnable can be stated before training. The point is not to teach a language model what a ten-line program already computes; it is that on such tasks we can tell which function the model learned, which is what a verifiable-reward pipeline for mathematics or code needs to know about its own supervision.

\emph{Answer-only} supervision (arm A) teaches a shortcut. On 4-digit primality the answer-trained model reaches \primeA\%, between a one-hot linear probe on the digits (\primeProbeOneHot\%) and the best cross-validated surface probe (\primeProbe\%), and its answers coincide with ``the last digit is 1, 3, 7 or 9'' on \primeAagreeLastDigitLo--\primeAagreeLastDigitHi\% of test items. Where no surface rule exists (divisibility by 7 or 13) it stays at chance at every training size we ran.

\emph{Trace} supervision (arm B: a deterministic procedure trace, then the answer) teaches the procedure once its steps are supervised often enough (Figure~\ref{fig:map}a). On divisibility by 7 the long-division trace takes accuracy from \divsevenBnSixty\% at $n=\nTrainSmall$ training examples to \divsevenBnNinety\%, \divsevenBnOneEighty\% and \divsevenBnTwoSeventy\% at 90, \nTrain{} and 270; on divisibility by 13, at the same trace length, from \divthirteenBnOneEighty\% at \nTrain{} to \divthirteenBnThreeSixty\% and \divthirteenBnFiveForty\% at 360 and 540. The trace works only as the input's own computation, and only if the model computes it before answering: a scrambled trace, a correct trace of a different number, and a trace placed after the answer all leave accuracy at chance, and prompting the base model does not come close.

What sets the dose? A trace for divisibility by $d$ writes one remainder per digit, so $n$ traces of $k$ steps supervise $m=kn/(10d)$ transitions per entry of the (remainder, digit) table. After our first preregistered account of trace accuracy failed (S5, compounding a fixed per-step accuracy over length), we preregistered that per-step accuracy follows $m$ (S7), a quantity known before training. Three of its predicted cells hold (Figure~\ref{fig:map}b); one fails because the step for $d=11$ is easier ($10\equiv-1\pmod{11}$), and a preregistered dose test on $d=13$ (S9) fails because its step is harder: at equal $m$, 13 trails 7. Supervision frequency drives trace learning within a task; step difficulty shifts the curve across tasks.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A controlled comparison of supervision formats under exact labels, with scrambled-trace, grounding, answer-first, shuffled-label and prompting controls and paired tests on problem-disjoint test sets (\S\ref{sec:setup}, \S\ref{sec:controls}).
\item Evidence that exact answer labels teach the surface probe's function rather than the verifier's (\S\ref{sec:shortcut}).
\item Dose-response evidence that exact traces teach the procedure once its steps are supervised often enough, with a predictor fixed before training and a confound, step difficulty, that we name (\S\ref{sec:traces}).
\item A preregistered record of ten tests, including three failures, all reported (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}).
\end{itemize}
One result reversed an earlier conclusion of ours. A verdict written on \priorVerdictDate{} from a \nTrainSmall-example trace run stated that traces do not unlock divisibility by 7; the preregistered \nTrain-example cell, which finished afterwards with the same code, gave \divsevenBsZero\%. The dose curve shows why both were right about their own $n$. The replication of that cell was a go/no-go condition for this submission, and it passed (\divsevenBsOne\% and \divsevenBsTwo\% in seeds 1 and 2).

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Intermediate steps as supervision.} Scratchpads \citep{nye2021show} and chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting \citep{wei2022chain} showed that emitting intermediate steps improves multi-step computation, and CoT helps mainly on mathematical and symbolic tasks \citep{sprague2025cot}. For small transformers trained from scratch, the format of arithmetic data changes sample efficiency sharply \citep{lee2024teaching}, and GCD predictions follow rules built from divisors of the number base \citep{charton2024gcd}, the kind of surface rule we probe for. Errors compound across the steps of compositional tasks \citep{dziri2023faith}. Filler tokens can replace a chain of thought on some tasks \citep{pfau2024dot}; our scrambled control keeps the tokens and removes their order, and on divisibility it removes the gain.

\paragraph{Theory.} Parity-like targets give gradient-based learners almost no signal from end labels \citep{shalevshwartz2017failures}. Sub-task decomposition makes otherwise unlearnable sequence tasks learnable \citep{wies2023subtask}; CoT raises the expressivity of bounded-depth transformers \citep{feng2023towards,li2024chain,merrill2024expressive}, and next-token predictors trained on CoT data are universal learners \citep{malach2024autoregressive}. Transformers can represent automata through shallow shortcuts \citep{liu2023shortcuts}. The globality degree of \citet{abbe2024far} bounds what transformers learn efficiently as inputs grow, and an inductive scratchpad breaks that barrier; teacher-forced intermediate parities make parity learnable \citep{kim2025parity}. Grokking studies how algorithmic tables such as modular arithmetic are learned from a fraction of their entries \citep{power2022grokking}. Section~\ref{sec:why} uses these results as intuition only.

\paragraph{Traces and verifiers in post-training.} Transformers trained from scratch on verifiable traces depend less on trace semantics than usually assumed \citep{valmeekam2026beyond}. Process supervision rewards steps rather than outcomes \citep{lightman2024verify}; program-generated logic corpora improve reasoning \citep{morishita2024logic}. STaR fine-tunes on self-generated rationales that reach verified answers \citep{zelikman2022star}; RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) drives recent reasoning models \citep{lambert2024tulu,deepseek2025r1}. Fine-tuning on model-generated positives can amplify spurious correlations \citep{setlur2024rl}; SFT and RL generalize differently \citep{chu2025sft}; RLVR mostly reweights what the base model already samples \citep{yue2025rl}. Our answer arm is an instance of shortcut learning \citep{geirhos2020shortcut}; exact labels do not prevent it.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Tasks.} Each task has an exact decision procedure, so labels are correct by construction; arms see the same prompts and labels and differ only in the completion (Table~\ref{tab:tasks}). \textbf{prime}: is a 4-digit $n$ prime? The trace performs trial division by the primes up to $\lfloor\sqrt n\rfloor$, one ``$n \bmod p = m$.'' step per prime, stopping at the first divisor. \textbf{div$d$}: is $n$ divisible by $d$? The trace performs long division, $r\leftarrow(10r+x_t)\bmod d$ for each digit $x_t$, for $d\in\{2,3,7,11,13\}$ with 4 digits and $d=7$ with 6. \textbf{valid}: does a propositional conclusion follow from its premises (Z3 \citep{demoura2008z3})? Arguments instantiate \nForms{} forms shared between training and test, so the task is surface-learnable by design (Appendix~\ref{app:logic}). Test sets have \nTest{} items (\nTestClass{} per class), are problem-disjoint from training and identical across arms; training sets are balanced samples of size $n$.

\paragraph{Arms.} \textbf{base}: zero-shot. \textbf{A}: ``Answer: Yes'' or ``Answer: No''. \textbf{B}: the trace, a newline, the answer (Appendix~\ref{app:formats}). \textbf{C}: B's traces permuted across examples and token-shuffled; the answer keeps the true label. \textbf{D} (S8): a fluent, correct trace of a \emph{different} number with the same label. \textbf{B$'$}: answer first, then the trace. \textbf{A-full}: answers on the whole training pool. \textbf{S} (S10): the base model's own sampled traces, kept when the exact gate accepts the final answer (STaR-style). \textbf{Shuffled labels}: from an earlier study with the same model and LoRA configuration. On the base model we also test a zero-shot CoT prompt and \nShots{} trace demonstrations.

\paragraph{Training and evaluation.} LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} with $r=\loraRank$, $\alpha=\loraAlpha$, dropout \loraDropout{} on the attention projections of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \citep{qwen2024qwen25}; AdamW, learning rate $\learningRate$, batch size 1, \nEpochs{} epochs, bf16; loss on completion tokens only. Evaluation is greedy generation with one answer extractor for every arm. A run takes \runMinLow--\runMinHigh{} minutes on one consumer GPU at $n=\nTrain$.

\paragraph{Statistics, preregistration and provenance.}
\label{sec:prereg}
Paired comparisons use the exact McNemar test \citep{mcnemar1947note} within a seed, with Holm correction \citep{holm1979simple} across tasks at $\alpha=\alphaLevel$; because McNemar counts test items, we also report seed SDs and bootstrap intervals over items and seeds. A first preregistration, frozen before a prior grid of \nPriorRuns{} runs, fixed four endpoints (H1: B beats A; H2: B beats C; H3: B is more efficient per token; H4: on div7 B reaches about 60\% with A at chance). The second was committed (\preregCommit) at \preregTime{} on \preregDate, before any of its runs, and fixes S1--S6; its decision log adds, each before the cells it governs had finished, a clarification of S5's primary cell (\sFiveClarTime), S7 (\sSevenTime), S8--S9 (\sEightNineTime) and S10 (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}). S3 and S6 were effectively settled before registration (S3 follows from the last-digit rule; A had already missed all \primeHardNegN{} hard negatives of the prior test set). The surface probe belongs to neither preregistration. \emph{Provenance}: the prior study's verdict (\priorVerdictDate) that traces do not unlock div7 was written from the \nTrainSmall-example run alone (\divsevenBsmall\%) while the preregistered \nTrain-example cell was still running; that cell finished at \divsevenBsZero\% with no code change.

\subsection{Exact answer labels teach the surface}
\label{sec:shortcut}
\paragraph{Answers track surface probes.} We fit logistic regression, a one-hidden-layer MLP and $k$-nearest neighbours on the same \nTrain{} training items as arm A, on \nProbeFeatures{} one-hot digit-by-position features alone or with engineered divisibility features (last digit even or 0/5; digit sum mod 3 and 9; alternating digit sum mod 11), or for arguments on atom-renamed word and character $n$-grams, and choose the probe by 5-fold cross-validation; on div2, div3 and div11 the engineered features contain the answer, so there the probe is one-hot only. A tracks the fair probe (Figure~\ref{fig:map}c): on prime it lies between the one-hot linear probe (\primeProbeOneHot\%) and the best probe (\primeProbe\%), on every divisibility task both are at chance, and on valid both are near ceiling.

\paragraph{The primality shortcut.} A's answers (recovered from per-item correctness) coincide with ``the last digit is 1, 3, 7 or 9'' on \primeAagreeLastDigitLo\% and \primeAagreeLastDigitHi\% of test items, and that rule alone scores \ruleLastDigit\%: A has learned divisibility by 2 and 5 and nothing else (\primeAprimesCorrect\% of primes, \primeAcompCorrect\% of composites correct). The rule mislabels every composite ending in 1, 3, 7 or 9; the test set has \primeCompThreeSevenN{} such composites with a factor 3 or 7, easy for any sieve, and A gets \primeCompThreeSevenA{} of them right (seeds 0 and 1). On a dedicated set of \primeHardNneg{} odd composites without a factor $\le 7$ and as many primes (S6, \verdictSSix, but settled in advance), A reaches \primeHardA\% and agrees with the rule on \primeHardAagree\% of items.

\paragraph{More answers do not buy the program.} On prime, A reaches \primeAsmall\%, \primeA\% and \primeAlarge\% at $n=\nTrainSmall$, \nTrain{} and \nTrainLarge{}, and \primeAtok\% on the full pool. On div7 it stays at chance at every $n$ (\divsevenAnSixty\%, \divsevenAnOneEighty\%, \divsevenAnFiveForty\%) and on div13 likewise (\divthirteenAnOneEighty\% and \divthirteenAnThreeSixty\% at \nTrain{} and 360); on div7 it falls below the zero-shot base (\divsevenBase\%; $p=\divsevenABasepsZero$, $\divsevenABasepsOne$, $\divsevenABasepsTwo$ for seeds 0--2), whose long free-form answers answer-only training removes. With labels permuted across items, the earlier study's answer arm sat at \shufMin--\shufMax\% on all seven of its tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}): correct labels drive A, and on primality what they buy is the rule.

\subsection{Exact traces teach steps seen often enough}
\label{sec:traces}
\paragraph{Divisibility by 7.} At $n=\nTrain$, over three seeds, B reaches \divsevenB\% (\divsevenBsZero, \divsevenBsOne, \divsevenBsTwo) against A's \divsevenA\% ($p=\divsevenBApsZero$, $\divsevenBApsOne$, $\divsevenBApsTwo$), and C stays at \divsevenC\% ($p\le\divsevenBCpMax$ against B). Seeds 1 and 2 are the preregistered replication (S1 \verdictSOne); a re-run of seed 0 reproduced \divsevenBsZeroRerun\%. The gain holds on both classes (\divsevenByes\% on multiples, \divsevenBno\% on non-multiples).

\paragraph{Dose.} With the trace format fixed, accuracy rises with the number of training traces while A stays flat (Figure~\ref{fig:map}a). Div7 B goes from \divsevenBnSixty\% at $n=\nTrainSmall$ to \divsevenBnNinety\% at 90 ($p=\pDivsevennNinety$), \divsevenBnOneEighty\% at \nTrain{} ($p=\pDivsevennOneEighty$) and \divsevenBnTwoSeventy\% at 270 ($p=\pDivsevennTwoSeventy$). Div13 B, with the same 4 steps, goes from \divthirteenBnOneEighty\% at \nTrain{} ($p=\pDivthirteennOneEighty$) to \divthirteenBnThreeSixty\% at 360 ($p=\pDivthirteennThreeSixty$) and \divthirteenBnFiveForty\% at 540 ($p=\pDivthirteennFiveForty$). At $n=\nTrain$, div13's failure is visible in its generations: \divthirteenTraceOK\% of traces are correct, the first error comes at step 2 in \divthirteenFirstErrTwo{} of \divthirteenNitems{} items, and \divthirteenWrongZero{} of \divthirteenWrongN{} wrong traces end at $r=0$, so the model answers ``yes'' \divthirteenPredYes\% of the time. In div7 generations, digits and carried remainders are copied without error (\divsevenDigitCopy\%, \divsevenCarry\%); every error is a wrong lookup of $(10r+x)\bmod 7$ (descriptively, lookups are right \stepLocalAcc{} of the time, $\stepLocalAcc^{4}=\stepLocalPowFour\%$ against \divsevenFullTrace\% error-free traces observed).

\paragraph{The predictor fixed before training (S7) and its confound.} We preregistered that per-step accuracy $p$ follows $m=kn/(10d)$, with five predicted cells, passing if four hold. Three hold: (a) div13 at $n=540$ ($m=\mDivthirteennFiveForty$): $p=\pDivthirteennFiveForty$, accuracy \divthirteenBnFiveForty\%; (b) div7 at $n=270$ ($m=\mDivsevennTwoSeventy$): $p=\pDivsevennTwoSeventy$; (c) div7 at $n=90$ ($m=\mDivsevennNinety$): $p=\pDivsevennNinety$, accuracy \divsevenBnNinety\%. Prediction (d) fails: div11 at $m=\mDiveleven$ reached $p=\pDiveleven$, above the predicted interval, because $10\equiv-1\pmod{11}$ makes its step a subtraction, $r'=(x-r)\bmod 11$ (div3, with $r'=(r+x)\bmod 3$, reaches \divthreeB\%). Prediction (e) awaits div2. S7's status, as computed by our analysis script, is \verdictSSeven{} (\SsevenHeld/5 hold, \SsevenPending{} pending). The preregistered dose test S9 \verdictSNine: div13 at $n=360$ ($m=\mDivthirteenMid$) reached \divthirteenBnThreeSixty\% against the required 80\% (A: \divthirteenAnThreeSixty\%), while div7 at the similar $m=\mDivseven$ reached \divsevenBnOneEighty\%. At equal $m$, div13 trails div7: its step, $r'=(x-3r)\bmod 13$, is harder, the same confound that failed (d) in the other direction. Within a task, supervision frequency drives trace learning; across tasks, step difficulty shifts the curve, and $m$ alone does not predict it.

\paragraph{Length (S5): failed as preregistered.} The prediction for 6-digit div7 was B $\approx p^6=\divsevenSixPred\%$, with $p=\stepAcc$ measured on 4-digit generations. The primary cell, B trained and tested on 6-digit traces, reached \divsevenSixBsZero\% and \divsevenSixBsOne\% in seeds 0 and 1, outside the 10-point tolerance: S5 \verdictSFive. Its own per-step accuracy is \pDivsevenSix, since at $k=6$ the same $n$ gives more transitions per entry ($m=\mDivsevenSix$). The secondary cell, which holds the 4-digit model fixed and applies it to 6-digit inputs, gives \divsevenTransferSixB\%, close to $p^6$: compounding describes length transfer for a fixed model, not what a model trained on longer traces learns. Forms that allow for a wrong trace ending in a right answer are post hoc (Appendix~\ref{app:compound}).

\paragraph{Primality: the trace hurts.} B reaches \primeB\% against A's \primeA\% ($p=\primeBApsZero$, $\primeBApsOne$, $\primeBApsTwo$ for seeds 0--2), and B vs.\ C is not significant ($p=\primeBCpsZero$, $\primeBCpsOne$). B classifies \primeBprimesCorrect\% of primes and \primeBcompCorrect\% of composites correctly, but scrambled C shows the same split in seed 1 (\primeCsOnePrimes/\nTestClass{} primes, \primeCsOneComps/\nTestClass{} composites), so the split reflects a bias toward ``composite'', not trace length. At $n=\nTrainLarge$, B reaches \primeBlarge\% against A's \primeAlarge\% ($p=\primeBAplarge$). A trial-division step is a table indexed by $(n,p)$ with more than $10^5$ entries, so $m\ll1$ at these $n$.

\paragraph{Validity and cost.} On valid both formats work (A \validA\%, B \validB\%, C \validC\%; B$-$A $p=\validBApsZero$, $\validBApsOne$, $\validBApsTwo$ for seeds 0--2). B's completions contain \tokRatioLow--\tokRatioHigh{} times as many tokens as A's (Qwen tokenizer). Of the first preregistration's endpoints, H1 held on div7 only, H2 on div7 and valid, H3 failed where answers work, and H4 held at $n=\nTrain$ but not $n=\nTrainSmall$.

\subsection{Controls}
\label{sec:controls}
\paragraph{The trace must be the input's own computation.} C (scrambled) sits at \divsevenC\% on div7 and \validC\% on valid. D, a fluent and correct trace of a different number with the right label, gives \divsevenDsZero\% and \divsevenDsOne\% on div7 (S8 \verdictSEight). What helps is the computation on this input, not trace-like text.

\paragraph{The trace must come before the answer.} B$'$, trained with the trace after the answer, gives \divsevenBpsZero\% and \divsevenBpsOne\% on div7, like A, although it still writes a trace after answering with per-step accuracy \pDivsevenBp. Training on traces placed after the answer does not move the procedure into the answer; the model has to compute the trace first.

\paragraph{Prompting does not replace training.} On the base model, a zero-shot CoT prompt gives \divsevenCot\% on div7, \primeCot\% on prime and \validCot\% on valid; \nShots{} trace demonstrations give \divsevenFew\%, \primeFew\% and \validFew\%. No prompt approaches trace-trained B on div7 (\divsevenB\%), so training, not inference-time reasoning alone, carries the gain. Arm S (S10, the base model's own gate-filtered traces) gives \divsevenSelfTrace\% on div7 (\verdictSTen).

\section{An automaton view (intuition)}
\label{sec:why}
\begin{remark}[intuition, not a theorem]
\label{rem:automaton}
Let $x_1\cdots x_k$ be the digits of $n$ and $\delta_d(r,a)=(10r+a)\bmod d$. Then $d\mid n$ iff $\delta_d^{*}(0,x_1\cdots x_k)=0$: divisibility by $d$ is recognized by a $d$-state automaton reading digits. The arm-B trace writes its states. Under teacher forcing each supervised state is a function of two tokens already in the context, so $n$ traces are $kn$ samples of one table with $10d$ entries, $m=kn/(10d)$ per entry. Answer-only supervision gives $n$ samples of the $k$-fold composition, and for $d$ coprime to 10 the label is nearly independent of every proper subset of the digits: for $d=7$ and $k=\divsevenSteps$, fixing any three digits leaves $\Pr(7\mid n)$ between \condLow{} and \condHigh{} (marginal \condMarg).
\end{remark}
The remark resembles the globality barrier of \citet{abbe2024far} and the teacher-forced CoT of \citet{kim2025parity}, but those are statements about learning as the input length grows and do not apply literally to fixed 4-digit inputs. It counts how often each transition is supervised, not how hard it is: for $d=3$ and $d=11$ the transition is an addition or subtraction mod $d$, for $d=7$ and $d=13$ it involves a multiplication, and the results follow difficulty as well as $m$. Whether transformers learn such tables through the kind of shortcut \citet{liu2023shortcuts} describe, and how the rate depends on coverage of the table, as in grokking \citep{power2022grokking}, is what a model of the dose curve would have to explain.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What exact labels guarantee.} An exact verifier guarantees that no training label is wrong, not that the labels identify the verifier's function among those a model can fit. The rule A learned on primality calls every composite ending in 1, 3, 7 or 9 prime; by construction that includes all \hardNegPop{} of the \compPop{} 4-digit composites without a factor $\le 7$. Shuffled labels show that the gain comes from label correctness but cannot tell the verifier's function from a surface rule; inputs on which the rule is wrong can.

\paragraph{Supervision design for verifiable-reward pipelines.} (1) Fit a cheap surface probe on the verified data before training; where it is accurate, answer supervision will teach its function, so evaluate where it is wrong. (2) A correct trace is not enough: budget how often each step type is supervised, expect harder steps to need more, and check per-step accuracy on held-out items after a small run. (3) The trace must be the input's own computation and must precede the answer. (4) Do not read a null from one training size: our \nTrainSmall-example null on div7 was the bottom of a dose curve.

\paragraph{RLVR: a hypothesis, not a result.} With a binary verifier the RLVR reward depends on the final answer only. We hypothesize, without testing, that RLVR then behaves like answer supervision with on-policy sampling: on a surface-learnable task a surface rule already earns most of the reward (the last-digit rule is right on \ruleLastDigit\% of our prime test items), and on div7 the policy would find the procedure only if its own samples sometimes execute it \citep{yue2025rl}. We ran no RL; arm S (S10) is the nearest experiment.

\paragraph{Limitations.} One model family, with 1.5B as the main size, and one LoRA configuration. Binary tasks with \nTest{} test items; McNemar $p$-values count items, and with one to three seeds per cell the replication-level evidence is thinner than they suggest (div7 B has seed SD \divsevenBsd{} points, valid B \validBsd). The dose curves have one seed per $n$ except $n=\nTrain$. One trace format per task. S5 and S9 failed; S7 is pending with one failed cell, and $m$ is confounded with step difficulty. S3 and S6 were settled before registration. The shuffled-label control comes from an earlier study; the probe was defined after the prior grid; the logic task shares its forms between training and test.

\section{The logic task}
\label{app:logic}
Arguments instantiate \nFormsHalf{} valid forms (modus ponens, modus tollens, hypothetical syllogism, disjunctive syllogism, constructive dilemma, contraposition) and \nFormsHalf{} invalid ones (affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, affirming a disjunct, a broken chain, the converse, and ``or'' to ``and'') with distinct random atom letters, rendered in English (``if $A$ then $B$'', ``not $A$'', ``$A$ or $B$''). The schema proposes an argument and Z3 decides it: the argument is labeled valid iff the premises together with the negated conclusion are unsatisfiable. For a valid argument the trace names the unsatisfiable core; for an invalid one it gives the satisfying assignment as a countermodel. Training and test share the \nForms{} forms and never an instance, so a model can reach high accuracy by recognizing the form, which the atom-renamed $n$-gram probe does (\validProbe\%). The task therefore checks that answers work when the surface determines the label; it is not a test of logical reasoning. Its results: base \validBase\%, A \validA\% (\validAsZero{} and \validAsOne), B \validB\% (\validBsZero{} and \validBsOne), C \validC\% (\validCsZero{} and \validCsOne); at $n=\nTrainSmall$, A \validAsmall\% and B \validBsmall\%; at $n=\nTrainLarge$, both \validAlarge\%.

\section{The compounding approximation (post hoc)}
\label{app:compound}
This derivation is post hoc: it was not part of the preregistered S5 rule, and it does not change S5's verdict. Suppose each emitted state is correct with probability $p$, independently, and that after the first error the emitted final state is uniform on $\mathbb{Z}_d$. With probability $p^k$ the trace is error-free and the answer is right. Otherwise the answer is right with probability $1/d$ on a multiple of $d$ and $(d-1)/d$ otherwise, so on a balanced test set the accuracy is $p^k+\tfrac12(1-p^k)\,[\tfrac1d+\tfrac{d-1}d]=(1+p^k)/2$. Both assumptions can fail: errors are correlated across steps (a model that miscomputes one table entry does so whenever the entry recurs), and a wrong state need not be uniform (on div13, wrong traces end at $r=0$ in \divthirteenWrongZero{} of \divthirteenWrongN{} cases). The positive-class accuracy, $p^k+(1-p^k)/d$, is the cleaner check, because a model that answers ``no'' by default scores well on non-multiples: on 6-digit div7 it is \divsevenSixByes\%.
