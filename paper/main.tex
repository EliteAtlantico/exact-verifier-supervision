\begin{abstract}
Exact verifiers (primality tests, SAT solvers, unit tests) label data with no noise at no marginal cost, and they supply the rewards of verifiable-reward post-training. We use them as a measurement instrument: with label noise removed, the effect of the supervision \emph{format} can be isolated. We fine-tune Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with LoRA on \nTrain{} exactly labeled examples per task and compare supervision by the answer alone with supervision by a deterministic procedure trace followed by the answer, against a scrambled-trace control. Answer-only training recovers about what simple probes on the input's surface recover: on 4-digit primality it reaches \primeA\%, and its answers coincide with the rule ``the last digit is 1, 3, 7 or 9'' on \primeAagreeLastDigitLo--\primeAagreeLastDigitHi\% of test items. On 4-digit divisibility by 7, which has no surface rule, answers stay at chance (\divsevenA\%, below the zero-shot base at \divsevenBase\%), while a \divsevenSteps-step long-division trace reaches \divsevenB\% over three seeds and its scrambled counterpart \divsevenC\%. A short correct trace is not enough: at the same length, divisibility by 13 reaches \divthirteenB\% with \divthirteenTraceOK\% of its traces correct, and on primality traces hurt (\primeB\%). Our preregistered prediction that trace accuracy compounds a fixed per-step accuracy over the trace length failed: 6-digit divisibility by 7 reached \divsevenSixB\% against a predicted \divsevenSixPred\%. A successor hypothesis, preregistered before its cells ran, ties per-step accuracy to how often each (remainder, digit) transition is supervised; its first predicted cell disagrees with it, because step difficulty varies with the divisor, and its other tests are pending. The lesson concerns how to design supervision for verifiable-reward pipelines, not arithmetic.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Verifiable-reward post-training rests on programs that decide correctness exactly: answer checkers for mathematics, unit tests for code, solvers for logic \citep{lambert2024tulu,deepseek2025r1}. Such verifiers are often described as a source of perfect labels for free. They are perfect in one sense: a verified label is never wrong. Whether a model trained on those labels learns the computation the verifier performs, or a cheaper function that agrees with it on the training distribution, is a separate question, and in practice it is confounded with label noise, with task difficulty, and with the format of the supervision.

We use exact verifiers to remove the first confound. When every label is correct, any difference between two training regimes on the same inputs is a difference in what the supervision exposes. We choose small decision tasks whose structure is known in advance (which surface rules approximate the label, which steps the natural procedure takes) so that what should be learnable can be stated before training. The point is not to teach a language model what a ten-line program already computes. It is that on such tasks we can tell which function the model learned, which is what a verifiable-reward pipeline for mathematics or code needs to know about its own supervision and usually cannot measure.

We compare two supervision formats on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct. \emph{Answer-only} supervision (arm A) teaches a shortcut. On 4-digit primality the answer-trained model reaches \primeA\%, between a one-hot linear probe on the digits (\primeProbeOneHot\%) and the best cross-validated surface probe (\primeProbe\%). Its answers are a one-token rule: they coincide with ``the last digit is 1, 3, 7 or 9'' on \primeAagreeLastDigitLo--\primeAagreeLastDigitHi\% of test items, and that rule alone scores \ruleLastDigit\%. On divisibility by 7, where no surface rule exists, it stays at chance. \emph{Trace} supervision (arm B: a deterministic procedure trace, then the answer) can teach what answers cannot: the \divsevenSteps-step long-division trace lifts divisibility by 7 from \divsevenA\% to \divsevenB\% over three seeds, while a scrambled trace with the same tokens (arm C) stays at \divsevenC\%. A correct trace is not sufficient, and neither is a short one. With the same \divsevenSteps{} steps, divisibility by 13 barely moves (\divthirteenB\% vs.\ \divthirteenA\%, one seed), and on primality traces hurt (\primeB\% vs.\ \primeA\%).

Our preregistered account was that trace supervision works when an error-free trace is likely: with per-step accuracy $p$ over $k$ steps, about $p^k$. Its test failed (S5). Trained on 6-digit traces, the model reached \divsevenSixB\% against the predicted \divsevenSixPred\%, because its own per-step accuracy (\pDivsevenSix) exceeded the 4-digit model's (\pDivseven). Since $p$ is measured after training, $p^k$ describes a trained model; it cannot say in advance which supervision will work. After the failure, and before its cells ran, we preregistered a quantity that can be computed before training (S7): the number of supervised transitions per entry of the (remainder, digit) table, $m=kn/(10d)$ for $n$ traces of $k$ steps modulo $d$. The three cells observed when we wrote it down are ordered by $m$ (Figure~\ref{fig:map}a), but the first cell predicted in advance contradicts it: divisibility by 11 at $m=\mDiveleven$ reached $p=\pDiveleven$, above the predicted interval. Its steps are easier, because $10\equiv-1 \pmod{11}$ turns each step into a subtraction. Step difficulty varies with $d$ and confounds $m$; the remaining tests of S7, a dose test at fixed $d$ (S9) and a grounding control (S8) are reported as they come in.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A controlled comparison of supervision formats under exact labels (answers, procedure traces, scrambled traces, shuffled labels) with paired tests on problem-disjoint test sets (\S\ref{sec:setup}).
\item Evidence that exact answer labels teach the surface probe's function rather than the verifier's: probe-level accuracy, a last-digit rule that matches the model's answers item by item, and failure on every composite the rule mislabels (\S\ref{sec:shortcut}).
\item Evidence on when exact traces teach: the trace unlocks divisibility by 7 through its content, not its tokens; the same format fails for divisibility by 13 and for primality; generation-level diagnostics show where (\S\ref{sec:traces}).
\item A preregistered record that includes a failed prediction (S5), a successor (S7) whose first predicted cell failed, and tests still running, all reported (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}).
\end{itemize}
One result reversed an earlier conclusion of ours. A verdict written on \priorVerdictDate{} from a \nTrainSmall-example trace run stated that traces do not unlock divisibility by 7; the preregistered \nTrain-example cell, which finished afterwards with the same code, gave \divsevenBsZero\%. We report both. Before any new run started, the replication of that cell was made a go/no-go condition for this submission, and it passed: \divsevenBsOne\% and \divsevenBsTwo\% in seeds 1 and 2 (\S\ref{sec:traces}).

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Intermediate steps as supervision.} Scratchpads \citep{nye2021show} and chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting \citep{wei2022chain} showed that emitting intermediate steps improves multi-step computation, and \citet{sprague2025cot} find the benefit of CoT concentrated on mathematical and symbolic tasks. For small transformers trained from scratch, \citet{lee2024teaching} show that the format of arithmetic data changes sample efficiency sharply, and \citet{charton2024gcd} explains a transformer's GCD predictions by rules built from divisors of the number base, the kind of surface rule we probe for. \citet{dziri2023faith} show how errors compound across the steps of compositional tasks. Filler tokens can replace a chain of thought on some tasks \citep{pfau2024dot}; our scrambled control keeps the tokens and removes their order, and on divisibility it removes the gain.

\paragraph{Theory.} Parity-like targets give gradient-based learners almost no signal from end labels \citep{shalevshwartz2017failures}. Sub-task decomposition makes otherwise unlearnable sequence-to-sequence tasks learnable \citep{wies2023subtask}; CoT raises the expressivity of bounded-depth transformers \citep{feng2023towards,li2024chain,merrill2024expressive}, and next-token predictors trained on CoT data are universal learners \citep{malach2024autoregressive}. Transformers can represent automata through shallow shortcuts \citep{liu2023shortcuts}. \citet{abbe2024far} define the globality degree of a target, show that it bounds what transformers learn efficiently as the input grows, and show that an inductive scratchpad breaks that barrier; \citet{kim2025parity} prove that teacher-forced intermediate parities make parity learnable. Grokking studies learning of algorithmic tables, such as modular arithmetic, from a fraction of their entries \citep{power2022grokking}. Section~\ref{sec:why} uses these results as intuition only.

\paragraph{Traces and verifiers in post-training.} \citet{valmeekam2026beyond} train transformers from scratch on formally verifiable traces and find the semantics of the trace less important than usually assumed. Process supervision rewards steps rather than outcomes \citep{lightman2024verify}, and program-generated logic corpora improve LLM reasoning \citep{morishita2024logic}. STaR fine-tunes on self-generated rationales that reach verified answers \citep{zelikman2022star}; RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) drives recent reasoning models \citep{lambert2024tulu,deepseek2025r1}. Fine-tuning on model-generated correct solutions can amplify spurious correlations \citep{setlur2024rl}; SFT and RL generalize differently \citep{chu2025sft}; and RLVR mostly reweights what the base model already samples \citep{yue2025rl}. Our answer arm is an instance of shortcut learning \citep{geirhos2020shortcut}; exact labels do not prevent it.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Exact verifiers as an instrument.} Each task has an exact decision procedure: trial division for primality, the remainder for divisibility, and Z3 \citep{demoura2008z3} for validity (premises entail $C$ iff premises $\wedge\,\neg C$ is unsatisfiable). Labels are correct by construction, every arm sees the same prompts and labels, and arms differ only in the completion the model is trained to produce.

\paragraph{Tasks.} Table~\ref{tab:tasks} summarizes them. \textbf{prime}: is a uniformly drawn 4-digit $n$ prime? The trace performs trial division by the primes up to $\lfloor\sqrt n\rfloor$, one ``$n \bmod p = m$.'' step per prime, and stops at the first divisor, so a prime needs \primeStepsMin--\primeStepsMax{} steps and a composite a median of \primeStepsMedComp. \textbf{div$d$}: is $n$ divisible by $d$? The trace performs long division, $r\leftarrow(10r+x_t)\bmod d$ for each digit $x_t$, so $k$ equals the number of digits; we use $d\in\{2,3,7,11,13\}$ with 4 digits and $d=7$ with 6 digits. \textbf{valid}: does a propositional conclusion follow from its premises? Arguments instantiate \nForms{} forms (\nFormsHalf{} valid, \nFormsHalf{} fallacies) with random atom letters, and the trace is the Z3 certificate; train and test share forms, so this task is surface-learnable by design (Appendix~\ref{app:logic}). Each test set has \nTest{} items (\nTestClass{} per class), is problem-disjoint from training and identical across arms; training sets are balanced samples of $n\in\{\nTrainSmall,\nTrain,\nTrainLarge\}$ (and, for S7 and S9, other $n$) from a training pool.

\paragraph{Arms.} \textbf{base}: the model zero-shot. \textbf{A}: the completion is ``Answer: Yes'' or ``Answer: No''. \textbf{B}: the deterministic trace, a newline, then the answer (Appendix~\ref{app:formats}). \textbf{C} (scrambled trace): B's traces are permuted across training examples, so that trace length cannot signal the label, then token-shuffled within each trace; the answer line keeps the example's true label (token totals within \cTokDiff\% of B's). \textbf{A-full}: answers on the whole training pool (\primeAtokN{} examples for prime, \validPool{} for valid). \textbf{Shuffled labels}: from an earlier study with the same model and LoRA configuration (\priorTrainN{} training items). Preregistered additions: \textbf{B$'$} (answer, then trace) on div7, whose answer cannot be computed by its trace; \textbf{D} (S8), a fluent, correct long-division trace of a \emph{different} number with the same label; and, on the base model, a zero-shot CoT prompt and \nShots-shot trace demonstrations. \textbf{S} (self-generated, STaR-style; not a decision rule): the base model samples traces, the exact gate keeps those whose answer is correct, and the model is fine-tuned on them.

\paragraph{Training and evaluation.} LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} with $r=\loraRank$, $\alpha=\loraAlpha$, dropout \loraDropout{} on the attention projections of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \citep{qwen2024qwen25}; AdamW, learning rate $\learningRate$, batch size 1, \nEpochs{} epochs, bf16; loss on completion tokens only. Evaluation is greedy generation, and one extractor serves every arm: the last ``Answer: Yes/No'', else the first standalone yes/no. Each run is a fresh process on one consumer GPU and takes \runMinLow--\runMinHigh{} minutes at $n=\nTrain$.

\paragraph{Statistics, preregistration and provenance.}
\label{sec:prereg}
Paired comparisons use the exact McNemar test \citep{mcnemar1947note} on per-item correctness within a seed, with Holm correction \citep{holm1979simple} across tasks at $\alpha=\alphaLevel$; they treat test items as the unit, so we also report seed SDs and bootstrap intervals over items and seeds. Two preregistrations govern the paper. The first was frozen before the prior grid of \nPriorRuns{} runs and fixed four endpoints: B beats A (H1), B beats C (H2), B is more efficient per training token (H3), and on div7 B reaches about 60\% with A at chance (H4). The second was committed (\preregCommit) at \preregTime{} on \preregDate, before any of its runs started, and fixes S1--S6 (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}). Its decision log adds, each before the cells it governs had finished, a clarification of S5's primary cell (\sFiveClarTime), S7 (\sSevenTime) and S8--S9 (\sEightNineTime). S3 and S6 add little evidence: S3 follows from the last-digit rule, and S6 was effectively settled before it was registered, since A already missed all \primeHardNegN{} hard negatives of the prior test set. The surface probe belongs to neither preregistration. \emph{Provenance.} The prior study's written verdict (\priorVerdictDate) that traces do not unlock div7 was written from the \nTrainSmall-example trace run alone (\divsevenBsmall\%) while the preregistered \nTrain-example cell was still running; that cell finished at \divsevenBsZero\% with no code change.

\subsection{Exact answer labels teach the surface}
\label{sec:shortcut}
\paragraph{Answers track surface probes.} We fit simple probes on the same \nTrain{} training items as arm A and score them on the test set: logistic regression, a one-hidden-layer MLP and $k$-nearest neighbours, on \nProbeFeatures{} one-hot digit-by-position features alone or with engineered divisibility features (last digit even; last digit 0 or 5; digit sum mod 3 and mod 9; alternating digit sum mod 11); for arguments, word and character $n$-grams with atoms renamed by first appearance. Hyperparameters and the reported probe are chosen by 5-fold cross-validation on the training items; on div2, div3 and div11 the engineered features contain the answer, so there the probe uses one-hot features only. A tracks the probe wherever the probe is fair (Figure~\ref{fig:map}b): on prime A lies between the one-hot linear probe (\primeProbeOneHot\%) and the best probe (\primeProbe\%), on div7 both are at chance, and on valid both are near ceiling.

\paragraph{The primality shortcut.} A's answers (recovered from per-item correctness) coincide with ``the last digit is 1, 3, 7 or 9'' on \primeAagreeLastDigitLo\% and \primeAagreeLastDigitHi\% of test items in seeds 0 and 1, and that rule alone scores \ruleLastDigit\%, within a point of A. A has learned divisibility by 2 and 5, the divisors visible in the last digit: it calls \primeAprimesCorrect\% of primes prime and gets \primeAcompCorrect\% of composites right. The rule mislabels every composite ending in 1, 3, 7 or 9, whatever its factors. The test set has \primeCompThreeSevenN{} such composites with a factor 3 or 7, easy for any sieve, and A gets \primeCompThreeSevenA{} of them right (seeds 0 and 1); it also misses all \primeHardNegN{} odd composites without a factor $\le 7$ (correct: \primeHardNegAcorrect). On the same test set, ``odd'' scores \ruleOdd\%, ``no factor 2 or 3'' \ruleNoTwoThree\% and ``no factor $\le 7$'' \ruleNoSeven\%. The preregistered S6 evaluates the prime adapters on \primeHardN{} numbers, \primeHardNneg{} hard negatives and as many primes, on which the rule calls every item prime: A reaches \primeHardA\% (\primeHardAneg\% on the hard negatives; B: \primeHardB\%; S6 \verdictSSix).

\paragraph{More answers do not buy the program.} On prime, A reaches \primeAsmall\%, \primeA\% and \primeAlarge\% at $n=\nTrainSmall$, \nTrain{} and \nTrainLarge{}, and \primeAtok\% with the full pool. On div7, A is at chance at every $n$ (\divsevenAsmall\%, \divsevenA\% and \divsevenAlarge\%) and learns a seed-dependent constant answer (seed 0: \divsevenAyessZero\% correct on multiples of 7, \divsevenAnosZero\% on non-multiples; seed 1: \divsevenAyessOne\% and \divsevenAnosOne\%). It is also below the zero-shot base (\divsevenBase\%; McNemar $p=\divsevenABasepsZero$, $\divsevenABasepsOne$ and $\divsevenABasepsTwo$ for seeds 0--2): the base model answers after long free-form responses (its evaluation on prime takes \baseEvalRatio{} times as long as A's), which answer-only training removes. Part of what a trace adds may therefore be test-time computation; B$'$ and the prompting controls (\S\ref{sec:controls}) separate the two. With labels permuted across training items, the earlier study's answer arm sat at \shufMin--\shufMax\% on all seven of its tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}), so correct labels are what drive A; on primality, what they buy is the rule.

\subsection{When exact traces teach}
\label{sec:traces}
\paragraph{Divisibility by 7.} Over three seeds B reaches \divsevenB\% (\divsevenBsZero, \divsevenBsOne, \divsevenBsTwo) against A's \divsevenA\% (\divsevenAsZero, \divsevenAsOne, \divsevenAsTwo; $p=\divsevenBApsZero$, $\divsevenBApsOne$, $\divsevenBApsTwo$), and C stays at \divsevenC\% ($p\le\divsevenBCpMax$ against B). Seeds 1 and 2 are the preregistered replication (S1 \verdictSOne), and a re-run of seed 0 with the generation-saving worker reproduced \divsevenBsZeroRerun\%. The gain holds on both classes (\divsevenByes\% on multiples, \divsevenBno\% on non-multiples). At $n=\nTrainSmall$ the same arm was at \divsevenBsmall\% (one seed).

\paragraph{What the traces get right and wrong (descriptive).} In the saved generations, every div7 trace has all \divsevenSteps{} steps, digits and carried remainders are copied without error (\divsevenDigitCopy\% and \divsevenCarry\%), and every error is a wrong lookup of $(10r+x)\bmod 7$. The lookups are right \stepLocalAcc{} of the time; independent errors would leave $\stepLocalAcc^{\divsevenSteps}=\stepLocalPowFour\%$ of traces error-free, and \divsevenFullTrace\% are. When a trace contains an error the answer is still right \divsevenAccGivenWrong\% of the time, since a wrong remainder is usually still non-zero. These are measurements of trained models, not predictions.

\paragraph{Divisibility by 13 and 11.} At the same length the trace fails for $d=13$: B \divthirteenB\% against A \divthirteenA\% ($p=\divthirteenBApsZero$, seed 0). Only \divthirteenTraceOK\% of its traces are correct; the first error comes at step 2 in \divthirteenFirstErrTwo{} of \divthirteenNitems{} items, and \divthirteenWrongZero{} of the \divthirteenWrongN{} wrong traces end at $r=0$, so the model answers ``yes'' \divthirteenPredYes\% of the time. For $d=11$ and $d=3$ the trace works (\divelevenB\% and \divthreeB\%, against A at \divelevenA\% and \divthreeA\%, seed 0), with per-step accuracy \pDiveleven{} for $d=11$. Their steps are easier: $10\equiv1\pmod 3$ and $10\equiv-1\pmod{11}$, so the update is $r'=(r+x)\bmod 3$ or $(x-r)\bmod 11$, whereas $d=7$ and $d=13$ require a multiplication ($r'=(3r+x)\bmod 7$, $(x-3r)\bmod 13$). S2 and S3 are in Table~\ref{tab:prereg}.

\paragraph{Length (S5): failed as preregistered.} The preregistered prediction for 6-digit div7 was B $\approx p^6=\divsevenSixPred\%$, with $p=\stepAcc$ measured on the 4-digit B generations. The primary cell, B trained and tested on 6-digit traces, reached \divsevenSixB\% (A: \divsevenSixA\%), outside the 10-point tolerance, so S5 \verdictSFive. The 6-digit model's own per-step accuracy is \pDivsevenSix: $p$ is not a fixed property of the task but depends on the training set. Two forms that allow for a wrong trace ending in a right answer, $(1+p^6)/2=\divsevenSixPredBal\%$ and $p^6+(1-p^6)g=\divsevenSixPredGuess\%$ with $g$ the 4-digit guess rate, were written down after the S5 cell was specified; they are post hoc and do not change the verdict. The secondary cell, the 4-digit adapter on 6-digit inputs, gives \divsevenTransferSixB\%.

\paragraph{Transitions per table entry (S7, pending).} Because $p$ is measured after training, we preregistered, after S5 failed and before any of its cells ran, a quantity fixed before training: $m=kn/(10d)$, the number of supervised transitions per entry of the (remainder, digit) table. The three cells observed at registration are ordered by $m$: div13 ($m=\mDivthirteen$, $p=\pDivthirteen$), div7 ($\mDivseven$, $\pDivseven$) and 6-digit div7 ($\mDivsevenSix$, $\pDivsevenSix$). S7 predicts five further cells and passes if four hold (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}). The first to finish, div11 at $m=\mDiveleven$, reached $p=\pDiveleven$, above the predicted interval (0.566, 0.955): prediction (d) \verdictSSevenD. Step difficulty varies with $d$ and confounds $m$, so a test of $m$ must hold $d$ fixed, as predictions (a)--(c) and S9 (div13 at $n=360$, $m=\mDivthirteenMid$: B $\ge 80\%$, A $\le 60\%$) do. S7 \verdictSSeven; S9 \verdictSNine. We do not claim S7.

\paragraph{Primality: the trace hurts.} B reaches \primeB\% against A's \primeA\% ($p=\primeBApsZero$ and $\primeBApsOne$), and B vs.\ C is not significant ($p=\primeBCpsZero$ and $\primeBCpsOne$): the trace's content gives no detectable benefit over its scrambled tokens. B classifies \primeBprimesCorrect\% of primes and \primeBcompCorrect\% of composites correctly, but scrambled C shows the same split in seed 1 (\primeCsOnePrimes/\nTestClass{} primes, \primeCsOneComps/\nTestClass{} composites), so the split reflects a learned bias toward ``composite'' rather than evidence about trace length. At $n=\nTrainLarge$, B reaches \primeBlarge\% against A's \primeAlarge\% ($p=\primeBAplarge$).

\paragraph{Validity and cost.} On valid both formats work: A \validA\%, B \validB\% (\validBsZero\% and \validBsOne\% by seed), C \validC\% (Appendix~\ref{app:logic}). Under the Qwen tokenizer, which splits numbers into single digits, B's training completions contain \tokRatioLow--\tokRatioHigh{} times as many tokens as A's (\primeTokB{} vs.\ \tokA{} per draw on prime). \section{An automaton view (intuition)}
\label{sec:why}
\begin{remark}[intuition, not a theorem]
\label{rem:automaton}
Let $x_1\cdots x_k$ be the digits of $n$ and $\delta_d(r,a)=(10r+a)\bmod d$. Then $d\mid n$ iff $\delta_d^{*}(0,x_1\cdots x_k)=0$: divisibility by $d$ is recognized by a $d$-state automaton reading digits. The arm-B trace writes the states $r_1,\dots,r_k$. Under teacher forcing, each supervised state is a function of two tokens already in the context, so $n$ traces are $kn$ samples of one table with $10d$ entries, $m=kn/(10d)$ per entry (\divsevenStepSamples{} samples of \divsevenTableSize{} entries for div7 at $n=\nTrain$). Answer-only supervision gives $n$ samples of the $k$-fold composition, and for $d$ coprime to 10 the label is nearly independent of every proper subset of the digits: for $d=7$ and $k=\divsevenSteps$, fixing any three digits leaves $\Pr(7\mid n)$ between \condLow{} and \condHigh, against a marginal of \condMarg.
\end{remark}
This view resembles the globality barrier of \citet{abbe2024far} and the teacher-forced CoT of \citet{kim2025parity}, but those are statements about learning as the input length grows, and they do not apply literally to fixed 4-digit inputs; we use the analogy as intuition only. The view counts how often each transition is supervised but not how hard it is to learn: for $d=3$ and $d=11$ the transition is an addition or subtraction mod $d$, for $d=7$ and $d=13$ a multiplication, and the results of \S\ref{sec:traces} follow the difficulty as much as $m$. It also suggests why primality fails: a trial-division step is a table indexed by $(n,p)$ with more than $10^5$ entries, so $m\ll 1$ at $n=\nTrain$. Whether transformers learn such tables through the kind of shortcut \citet{liu2023shortcuts} describe, and how the rate depends on coverage of the table, as in grokking \citep{power2022grokking}, is what S7 and S9 begin to test. On valid the trace is a certificate whose first clause fixes the answer, so $k$ is effectively 1.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What exact labels guarantee.} An exact verifier guarantees that no training label is wrong, not that the labels identify the verifier's function among those a model can fit from $n$ examples. The rule A learned on primality calls every composite ending in 1, 3, 7 or 9 prime; by construction that includes all \hardNegPop{} of the \compPop{} 4-digit composites without a factor $\le 7$. A shuffled-label control shows that the gain comes from label correctness but cannot tell the verifier's function from a surface rule; inputs on which the probe or the rule is wrong can.

\paragraph{Supervision design for verifiable-reward pipelines.} (1) Fit a cheap surface probe on the verified data before training; where it is accurate, answer supervision will teach its function, so evaluate on inputs where it is wrong. (2) A correct trace can still fail (div13, primality); count how often each step type is supervised and measure per-step accuracy on held-out items after a small run. (3) Do not read a null from one training size; our own \nTrainSmall-example null on div7 was the edge of a transition.

\paragraph{RLVR: a hypothesis, not a result.} \emph{Hypothesis (untested).} With a binary verifier the RLVR reward depends on the final answer only, so RLVR is answer supervision with on-policy sampling; on a surface-learnable task a surface rule already earns most of the reward (the last-digit rule is right on \ruleLastDigit\% of our prime test items), and RLVR should find a shortcut of the same kind, while on div7 it should find the procedure only if the policy's own samples sometimes execute it, consistent with \citet{yue2025rl}. We ran no RL. Arm S, the self-generated STaR-style arm, is the nearest experiment we have (\divsevenSelfTrace\% on div7).

\paragraph{Limitations.} One model family, with 1.5B as the main size, and one LoRA configuration. Binary tasks with \nTest{} test items; the McNemar $p$-values count items, and with two or three seeds per cell the replication-level evidence is thinner than they suggest (div7 B has seed SD \divsevenBsd{} points, valid B \validBsd). Several divisor cells have one seed. One trace format per task, written by us. S5 failed; S7's first predicted cell failed, and its $m$ is confounded with step difficulty. S3 and S6 were effectively settled before registration. The shuffled-label control comes from an earlier study with a related protocol; the probe was defined after the prior grid; the logic task shares \nForms{} forms between training and test. Fine-tuning on answers also removes the base model's test-time reasoning.

\section{The logic task}
\label{app:logic}
Arguments instantiate \nFormsHalf{} valid forms (modus ponens, modus tollens, hypothetical syllogism, disjunctive syllogism, constructive dilemma, contraposition) and \nFormsHalf{} invalid ones (affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, affirming a disjunct, a broken chain, the converse, and ``or'' to ``and'') with distinct random atom letters, rendered in English (``if $A$ then $B$'', ``not $A$'', ``$A$ or $B$''). The schema proposes an argument and Z3 decides it: the argument is labeled valid iff the premises together with the negated conclusion are unsatisfiable. For a valid argument the trace names the unsatisfiable core; for an invalid one it gives the satisfying assignment as a countermodel. Training and test share the \nForms{} forms and never an instance, so a model can reach high accuracy by recognizing the form, which the atom-renamed $n$-gram probe does (\validProbe\%). The task therefore checks that answers work when the surface determines the label; it is not a test of logical reasoning. Its results: base \validBase\%, A \validA\% (\validAsZero{} and \validAsOne), B \validB\% (\validBsZero{} and \validBsOne), C \validC\% (\validCsZero{} and \validCsOne); at $n=\nTrainSmall$, A \validAsmall\% and B \validBsmall\%; at $n=\nTrainLarge$, both \validAlarge\%.

\section{The compounding approximation (post hoc)}
\label{app:compound}
This derivation is post hoc: it was not part of the preregistered S5 rule, and it does not change S5's verdict. Suppose each emitted state is correct with probability $p$, independently, and that after the first error the emitted final state is uniform on $\mathbb{Z}_d$. With probability $p^k$ the trace is error-free and the answer is right. Otherwise the answer is right with probability $1/d$ on a multiple of $d$ and $(d-1)/d$ otherwise, so on a balanced test set the accuracy is $p^k+\tfrac12(1-p^k)\,[\tfrac1d+\tfrac{d-1}d]=(1+p^k)/2$. Both assumptions can fail: errors are correlated across steps (a model that miscomputes one table entry does so whenever the entry recurs), and a wrong state need not be uniform (on div13, wrong traces end at $r=0$ in \divthirteenWrongZero{} of \divthirteenWrongN{} cases). The positive-class accuracy, $p^k+(1-p^k)/d$, is the cleaner check, because a model that answers ``no'' by default scores well on non-multiples: on 6-digit div7 it is \divsevenSixByes\%.
