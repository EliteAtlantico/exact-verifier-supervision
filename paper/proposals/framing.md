**Reframed thesis (proposed).** *Exact labels are not neutral supervision. Where a pretrained model already
computes the answer and no surface rule fits the labels, fine-tuning on verified answers replaces the computation
with a guess, and fine-tuning on the model's own verifier-filtered solutions keeps it. Exact procedure traces teach
the procedure's steps within a task once each entry of its state table is supervised often enough, where "often
enough" depends on how hard the step is. Our count-based predictions of that threshold held within tasks and failed
across tasks and lengths.*

## 1. New abstract (220 words when rendered; LaTeX with existing macros)

```latex
\begin{abstract}
With exact verifier labels, two ways of supervising the same inputs differ only in format. Format and the base
model's own computation then shape what is learned. We LoRA-fine-tune Qwen2.5 models (0.5B--7B) on exactly labeled
decision tasks, supervising either the verified answer alone or a deterministic procedure trace, then the answer.
Where the zero-shot model already computes the answer and no surface rule fits the labels, answer-only fine-tuning
erases the computation: at 3B, divisibility by 7 falls from \threeBdivsevenBase\% to \threeBdivsevenA\%, with a
similar drop at 7B, while fine-tuning on the model's own verifier-filtered solutions keeps it (\threeBdivsevenS\%).
Where a surface rule fits, answers teach the rule: on 4-digit primality, 1.5B answer-trained models agree with
``the last digit is 1, 3, 7 or 9'' on \primeAagreeLastDigitLo--\primeAagreeLastDigitHi\% of test items. Exact
traces teach a procedure's steps within a task once each entry of its state table is supervised often enough: at
1.5B, divisibility by 7 rises from \divsevenBnSixty\% at $n=\nTrainSmall$ examples to \divsevenBnTwoSeventy\% at
270. How often is enough depends on the step: dose predictions logged before their runs held within tasks but
failed across tasks and for longer inputs, and a prediction about self-generated traces failed. Traces are not
uniformly better: at 1.5B they hurt on primality (\primeB\% vs.\ \primeA\%), and trace-trained models can write
unfaithful steps behind right answers.
\end{abstract}
```

```latex
\section{Introduction}
Verifiable-reward post-training rests on programs that decide correctness exactly: answer checkers for mathematics,
unit tests for code, solvers for logic \citep{lambert2024tulu,deepseek2025r1}. Such verifiers are often described as
a source of perfect labels for free, and a verified label is never wrong. A label that is never wrong is still not
neutral. The same exact label can be delivered as a bare answer, as the procedure that produced it, or as the
model's own solution kept because the verifier accepted it, and a pretrained model can fit each of these with a
different function. We ask which function each format installs, and what happens to the computation the model
performed before fine-tuning. Because every label in every arm is exact, label noise cannot explain a difference
between arms trained on the same inputs: the difference is due to the supervision format.

The first finding is that exact answers can remove a computation the model already has. The 3B and 7B Qwen2.5
zero-shot models already decide divisibility by 7 (\threeBdivsevenBase\% and \sevenBdivsevenBase\%), and the 3B
model writes a direct division line in \threeBbasedivsevenDirect\% of its answers. After LoRA fine-tuning on
\nTrain{} verified answers, both are at chance (\threeBdivsevenA\% and \sevenBdivsevenA\%). Per item, the 3B answer
arm is wrong on \threeBdivsevenAlostsZero{} and \threeBdivsevenAlostsOne{} of the \threeBdivsevenBaseRightN{} test
items the base model answered correctly (two seeds), and the 7B arm on \sevenBdivsevenAlostsZero{} and
\sevenBdivsevenAlostsOne{} of \sevenBdivsevenBaseRightN: about half, the rate of a guess that replaces a working
computation. Fine-tuning instead on the model's own solutions, kept when the verifier accepts the final answer
(arm S, as in STaR \citep{zelikman2022star}), loses \threeBdivsevenSlostsZero{} and \threeBdivsevenSlostsOne{} of
those items at 3B and reaches \threeBdivsevenS\%; at 7B (one seed) it loses \sevenBdivsevenSlostsZero{} and stays
level with the base. The effect is conditional. Where a surface rule fits the labels, answer training raises
accuracy above the base and teaches the rule: on 4-digit primality, 1.5B answers agree with ``the last digit is 1, 3,
7 or 9'' on \primeAagreeLastDigitLo--\primeAagreeLastDigitHi\% of test items. And a foreign exact trace also
displaces part of the base computation at 3B (wrong on \threeBdivsevenBlostsZero{} and \threeBdivsevenBlostsOne{} of
the \threeBdivsevenBaseRightN{} items, \threeBdivsevenB\% at $n=\nTrain$) before more traces recover it
(\threeBdivsevenBnTwoSeventysZero\% at $n=270$, one seed). In our runs, the arm that preserved the computation was
the one trained on the model's own verifier-filtered solutions, not every arm that included a trace.

The second finding is that exact traces teach a procedure's steps one table entry at a time. Long division by $d$
runs a $d$-state automaton. Under teacher forcing, each supervised step is a lookup in a table of $10d$
(remainder, digit) entries, so $n$ traces of $k$ steps supervise each entry about $m=kn/(10d)$ times, a number known
before training. Within a task, accuracy follows $m$ (Figure~\ref{fig:map}): at 1.5B, div7 trace accuracy goes from
\divsevenBnSixty\% at $n=\nTrainSmall$ to \divsevenBnNinety\% at 90, \divsevenBnOneEighty\% at \nTrain{} and
\divsevenBnTwoSeventy\% at 270, and div13 goes from \divthirteenBnOneEighty\% to \divthirteenBnFiveForty\% between
$n=\nTrain$ and 540, while answer-only training stays at chance at every $n$. Two conditions hold on div7. The trace
has to be the input's own computation (a correct trace of another number gives \divsevenDsZero\% and
\divsevenDsOne\%), and it has to precede the answer (answer first: \divsevenBpsZero\% and \divsevenBpsOne\%).
Predictions logged before their runs show where this account stops. S7's three within-task cells held, but its
cross-task cell failed: div11 was learned above the predicted range, because $10\equiv-1 \pmod{11}$ makes its step
a subtraction. S9 failed as well: div13 at $m=\mDivthirteenMid$ reached \divthirteenBnThreeSixtysZero\% against the
required 80\%, while div7 at $m=\mDivseven$ reached \divsevenBnOneEighty\%. So step difficulty moves the threshold,
and $m$ does not transfer across tasks. S5 predicted that 6-digit accuracy would compound the 4-digit step accuracy
($q^6=\divsevenSixPred\%$). It failed (\divsevenSixBsZero\% and \divsevenSixBsOne\%) because at the same $n$,
6-digit traces supervise each entry more often. S10 predicted that the 1.5B model's own filtered solutions would
not teach div7 ($\le 65\%$) and would keep validity at $\ge 90\%$. Both clauses failed: div7 reached
\divsevenSelfTrace\% and \divsevenSsOne\% in two seeds, and validity \validS\%. Traces are also not uniformly
better. At 1.5B they hurt on primality (\primeB\% vs.\ \primeA\%) and answers match them on validity. At 3B the
primality trace arm scores \threeBprimeB\% while only \threeBprimeClaimAcc\% of its ``$n \bmod p = r$'' claims are
correct: a shortcut written in the form of a trace.
```

## 4. Why synthetic tasks (two sentences, for the end of the introduction or the start of Setup)

> Synthetic decision tasks are our microscope: for each one we know the exact state table its procedure walks (the
> $10d$ (remainder, digit) entries of long division) and the exact shortcut rules that approximate its label (on
> primality, ``the last digit is 1, 3, 7 or 9'' alone scores \ruleLastDigit\%), so we can say which function a
> fine-tuned model learned, not only how often it is right. Natural reasoning data offer neither an exact state
> table nor an enumerable set of shortcuts, so a difference between trace and answer supervision measured on them
> cannot be traced to the supervision format.

(`\ruleLastDigit` = 82.5.)

## 5. Optional: claim-boundary box (adapted from the New Axiom advice, rewritten to match the data)

*We claim:* (1) on tasks with no surface rule that fits the labels, answer-only fine-tuning on exact labels replaced
a zero-shot computation with a chance-level guess at 3B and 7B, and lowered accuracy below zero-shot at 1.5B on div7
and div3; (2) fine-tuning on the model's own verifier-filtered solutions kept that computation (3B, two seeds; 7B,
one seed); (3) within a divisibility task, more supervised transitions per table entry raised per-step accuracy.
*We do not claim:* that traces always help (prime at 1.5B, valid, 7B valid); that $m$ predicts the threshold across
tasks or lengths (S7d, S9 and S5 failed); that self-generated traces fail at 1.5B (S10 failed); that the answer-arm
failures are impossibility results under other hyperparameters; or anything about RL.

```python
import sys, os
```
